# Oracle Corporation (ORCL) — FY2026 Company Research Report

**Report date:** September 3, 2026\
**Primary filing:** Oracle Corporation Form 10-K for the fiscal year ended May 31, 2026\
**Decision call:** **INITIATE / SMALL POSITION**\
**Decision user:** Long-term public-equity investor focused on per-share intrinsic value, downside risk, and an adequate margin of safety.

## Executive view

Oracle's fiscal 2026 results show that the cloud-infrastructure growth story is real, and they help explain why I believe the potential upside is large enough to justify a position. However, the same results also show why I am keeping that position relatively small. Fiscal 2026 total revenue was **\$67.357 billion**, total cloud revenue was **\$33.989 billion**, and cloud-infrastructure revenue was **\$18.101 billion**, up from **\$10.234 billion** in fiscal 2025. That means cloud infrastructure grew approximately **77%** year over year and cloud revenue represented just over half of total company revenue. [**Source: Oracle FY2026 Form 10-K, Item 7, p. 44; Consolidated Statements of Operations, p. 65.**](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1341439/000119312526277521/orcl-20260531.htm)

The problem is that the growth required an extraordinary amount of reinvestment. Oracle generated **\$31.977 billion of operating cash flow**, but capital expenditures reached **\$55.663 billion**, producing reported free cash flow of **negative \$23.686 billion**. Oracle states that the rise in capital expenditures was primarily driven by data-center expansion and that it expects the upward capex trend to continue in fiscal 2027 and following years. [**Source: Oracle FY2026 Form 10-K, Item 7, Liquidity and Capital Resources, p. 54.**](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1341439/000119312526277521/orcl-20260531.htm)

**My judgment:** I have **initiated a position in Oracle**, but I am keeping the position relatively small. I think the upside is meaningful because the market may be undervaluing Oracle's cloud growth, backlog, and long-term earnings potential. At the same time, the size of the data-center buildout, negative free cash flow, additional financing needs, and execution risk make me unwilling to invest heavily at this stage. My view is that ORCL is **worth the bet, but not worth making a concentrated bet** until I have stronger evidence that the new capacity can earn attractive returns after depreciation, power, financing costs, and continued reinvestment. **[Author judgment based on the cited operating and cash-flow evidence.]**

I also think the market may currently be valuing Oracle below what the business is actually worth. A stock price is simply the price at which investors are willing to trade the shares today; intrinsic value is my estimate of the present value of the future cash flows that ultimately belong to shareholders after accounting for debt, reinvestment, financing needs, and dilution. Those two numbers can be very different. My current view is that ORCL is **likely undervalued**, because the stock has repriced sharply lower even while OCI revenue and contracted backlog have continued to expand. I have not yet completed the DCF needed to quantify that gap, so I treat undervaluation as a thesis to test rather than a proven valuation result. **[Author judgment; operating evidence sourced above. Market-price evidence is discussed in Section 6.]**

## 1. What is changing at Oracle

Oracle is increasingly becoming a cloud-infrastructure company rather than primarily an on-premise software company. Cloud-infrastructure revenue increased from **\$6.840 billion in fiscal 2024**, to **\$10.234 billion in fiscal 2025**, to **\$18.101 billion in fiscal 2026**. Total cloud revenue increased from **\$19.774 billion** in fiscal 2024 to **\$33.989 billion** in fiscal 2026. [**Source: Oracle FY2026 Form 10-K, Item 7, p. 44; Consolidated Statements of Operations, p. 65.**](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1341439/000119312526277521/orcl-20260531.htm)

The June 10, 2026 earnings release adds evidence that demand remained strong at the end of the fiscal year. Oracle reported **Q4 cloud-infrastructure revenue of \$5.8 billion, up 93%**, and **remaining performance obligations (RPO) of \$638 billion, up 363% year over year**. Oracle also said that much of the Q3 and Q4 RPO increase came from large-scale AI contracts and that **\$75 billion** of prepaid or customer-supplied GPU hardware associated with large AI contracts reduces the capital Oracle itself must raise for those projects. [**Source: Oracle Investor Relations, "Oracle Announces Record Q4 and FY 2026 Results," June 10, 2026.**](https://investor.oracle.com/investor-news/news-details/2026/Oracle-Announces-Record-Q4-and-FY-2026-Results-Driven-by-Cloud-Infrastructure--Cloud-Applications/)

**My judgment:** The RPO and Q4 growth make it harder to dismiss Oracle's AI/cloud demand as only hype. At the same time, RPO is not the same as recognized revenue, cash flow, or economic profit. The question I care about is not only whether customers want the capacity, but whether Oracle can build and finance that capacity at returns that justify the investment. **[Author judgment based on the sources above.]**

## 2. The capital-intensity problem

Oracle's balance sheet and financing activity show how expensive the expansion has become. At May 31, 2026, Oracle had **\$31.289 billion of cash and cash equivalents** and another **\$605 million of marketable securities**. The company had **\$130.105 billion of gross principal for senior notes and other borrowings**, compared with \$92.917 billion one year earlier. The carrying amount of notes payable and other borrowings was **\$129.541 billion**. [**Source: Oracle FY2026 Form 10-K, Consolidated Balance Sheet, p. 64; Note 6, p. 82.**](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1341439/000119312526277521/orcl-20260531.htm)

Oracle also reported **\$26.648 billion of non-current operating lease liabilities** and **\$4.954 billion of mandatory convertible preferred stock** on the May 31, 2026 balance sheet. Those items matter because a final enterprise-to-equity bridge should not automatically treat gross debt minus cash as the complete adjustment. [**Source: Oracle FY2026 Form 10-K, Consolidated Balance Sheet, p. 64.**](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1341439/000119312526277521/orcl-20260531.htm)

Using only disclosed gross principal debt and cash, the preliminary bridge adjustment is:

`Enterprise value - $130.105B debt + $31.289B cash = enterprise value - $98.816B`

If the **\$605 million of marketable securities** is later confirmed to be non-operating and treated as cash-like, the preliminary adjustment becomes:

`Enterprise value - $130.105B debt + $31.289B cash + $0.605B securities = enterprise value - $98.211B`

This is only a starting bridge, not a final equity-value calculation, because lease liabilities, preferred stock, dilution, and other non-operating or debt-like claims still need to be reconciled. **[Source for inputs: Oracle FY2026 Form 10-K, p. 64 and Note 6, p. 82. Interpretation is author judgment.]**

The financing need is not finished. In its June 10, 2026 earnings release, Oracle said it raised **\$43 billion of debt financing and \$5 billion of equity financing in fiscal 2026** and expected to raise approximately **\$40 billion through a combination of debt and equity financing in fiscal 2027**, including a previously announced \$20 billion at-the-market equity issuance. [**Source: Oracle Investor Relations, June 10, 2026.**](https://investor.oracle.com/investor-news/news-details/2026/Oracle-Announces-Record-Q4-and-FY-2026-Results-Driven-by-Cloud-Infrastructure--Cloud-Applications/)

**My judgment:** This does not mean the investment is bad. It means the return on the investment matters more. If Oracle can turn the capacity into durable, high-utilization cloud revenue with attractive margins, the spending can create value. If utilization, pricing, or demand disappoints, the same spending can destroy value because Oracle has already committed significant capital and financing. **[Author judgment.]**

## 3. What management said before — and whether it happened

### Fiscal 2024 10-K

In the fiscal 2024 10-K, management said the share of cloud-services revenue in total revenue had increased and that it expected the trend to continue. Cloud services represented **37% of total revenue in fiscal 2024**, up from 32% in fiscal 2023 and 25% in fiscal 2022. Management also said cloud-services and license-support expenses had been growing and that it expected that trend to continue in fiscal 2025 as Oracle expanded existing data-center capacity and entered new locations. [**Source: Oracle FY2024 Form 10-K, Item 7, pp. 37 and 47.**](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1341439/000095017024075605/orcl-20240531.htm)

That direction did happen. Fiscal 2025 cloud services represented **43% of total revenue**, and capital expenditures increased from **\$6.866 billion in fiscal 2024 to \$21.215 billion in fiscal 2025**. [**Source: Oracle FY2025 Form 10-K, Item 7, pp. 37-38; Consolidated Statements of Cash Flows, p. 67.**](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1341439/000095017025087926/orcl-20250531.htm)

### Fiscal 2025 10-K

In the fiscal 2025 10-K, management again said it expected cloud and license revenue to continue increasing because of growth in cloud services and said it would continue investing in Oracle Cloud Infrastructure capacity and geographic expansion. [**Source: Oracle FY2025 Form 10-K, Item 7, pp. 37-38.**](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1341439/000095017025087926/orcl-20250531.htm)

The fiscal 2026 results again show that the operating growth occurred. Cloud-infrastructure revenue rose to **\$18.101 billion from \$10.234 billion**, while total cloud revenue reached **\$33.989 billion**. However, capex simultaneously jumped to **\$55.663 billion**, more than double the fiscal 2025 level. [**Source: Oracle FY2026 Form 10-K, Item 7, pp. 44 and 54; Consolidated Statements of Operations, p. 65.**](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1341439/000119312526277521/orcl-20260531.htm)

**My judgment:** Management has so far followed through on the direction it described: cloud is becoming more important and Oracle is spending aggressively to support it. What is not yet proven is whether the return on that spending will be high enough. The historical check therefore strengthens both sides of my thesis: management's cloud-growth claims deserve more credibility, but the capital-intensity concern is also getting larger, not smaller. **[Author judgment based on FY2024-FY2026 filings.]**

## 4. What I still need to know before increasing my position

I would not increase my position based only on revenue growth. Two issues that are directly supported by Oracle's disclosures are especially important to my next decision:

-   **RPO conversion:** Oracle reported **\$638 billion of remaining performance obligations** at May 31, 2026. The company expects to recognize approximately **12% over the next twelve months, 34% during months 13-36, 34% during months 37-60, and the remainder thereafter**. That gives me a disclosed revenue-recognition timeline, but I still need to judge whether the resulting revenue will convert into enough cash flow and economic return to justify the investment. [**Source: Oracle FY2026 Form 10-K, Note 1, p. 72. Final sentence is author judgment.**](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1341439/000119312526277521/orcl-20260531.htm)
-   **Per-share dilution:** Oracle states that its **maximum potential dilution from all outstanding stock-based awards was 3.5% as of May 31, 2026**. Its Series D Mandatory Convertible Preferred Stock also converts no later than **January 15, 2029** into between **499.8126 and 624.7657 common shares per preferred share**, subject to the terms described in the filing. I need to incorporate both forms of potential dilution into my eventual per-share valuation before deciding whether to increase the position. [**Source: Oracle FY2026 Form 10-K, Item 7, p. 55; Note 10, p. 90. Final sentence is author judgment.**](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1341439/000119312526277521/orcl-20260531.htm)

These are not small details. They determine whether the cloud expansion creates value for common shareholders or merely creates a much larger company that requires much more capital. **[Author judgment.]**

## 5. Stock price versus what the company is actually worth

The market price of ORCL and Oracle's intrinsic value are not the same thing. The **stock price** is the price investors are willing to pay for one share at a specific moment. It can move because of earnings expectations, news, interest rates, positioning, sentiment, momentum, or fear. **Intrinsic value**, by contrast, is what I believe the underlying business is worth based on the future cash flows that can ultimately accrue to shareholders after accounting for reinvestment, debt, financing costs, and dilution. **[Author valuation framework.]**

This distinction matters especially for Oracle because the stock has gone through an extreme repricing. ORCL reached a 52-week high of **\$345.72 in September 2025** and later fell to a **\$114.50 52-week low on July 28, 2026**. By early September 2026 the shares had recovered into roughly the mid-\$150s, but they remained far below the 2025 peak. [**Source: Barchart ORCL Performance Report, accessed September 3, 2026.**](https://www.barchart.com/stocks/quotes/ORCL/performance)

I do **not** think the 2025 high automatically tells me what Oracle is worth. A previous market price is not an intrinsic-value estimate. However, I do think the magnitude of the decline is important because the underlying business did not decline by the same amount. Fiscal 2026 OCI revenue increased approximately **77%**, Q4 OCI revenue increased **93%**, and Oracle reported **\$638 billion of remaining performance obligations** at year-end. [**Sources: Oracle FY2026 Form 10-K, Item 7, p. 44; Oracle Investor Relations, June 10, 2026.**](https://investor.oracle.com/investor-news/news-details/2026/Oracle-Announces-Record-Q4-and-FY-2026-Results-Driven-by-Cloud-Infrastructure--Cloud-Applications/)

**My judgment:** I currently think ORCL is **undervalued**. My view is that the market has penalized Oracle heavily for its enormous capital requirements, financing needs, and negative free cash flow, but may be discounting too much of the value created if its contracted AI and cloud demand converts into high-utilization, recurring infrastructure revenue. That perceived gap between market price and intrinsic value is why I have already **initiated a position**. However, I have kept the position relatively small because I have not yet completed the DCF or fully adjusted enterprise-to-equity bridge needed to determine exactly how large the undervaluation is. I see enough potential upside to justify taking the risk, but not enough certainty to justify a heavy allocation. **[Author judgment based on the cited filing, backlog, financing, and market-price evidence.]**

The chart is useful because it suggests that the market may be starting to recognize some of the value I believe is being underpriced. **[Author judgment.]**

## 7. Falsification test

The question I want the next stage of research to answer is:

**What observable evidence would show that Oracle's accelerating cloud-infrastructure revenue is failing to earn an adequate return on the capital required to produce it?**

Evidence against the risk case would include OCI growth remaining strong while capex intensity falls and free cash flow improves materially. If Oracle later provides enough operating disclosure to evaluate utilization and incremental returns, I would also want those measures to support the conclusion that the new capacity is earning an adequate return on capital. **[Author-defined falsification criteria.]**

Evidence that would make the thesis worse would include materially slower OCI growth while capex, depreciation, interest expense, lease commitments, or equity dilution continue rising, especially if free cash flow remains deeply negative without a clear path to normalization. A technical breakdown back through the recent recovery range would not by itself disprove intrinsic undervaluation, but it would show that the market is not yet confirming my view. **[Author-defined falsification criteria.]**

## Final call — INITIATE / SMALL POSITION

**I have initiated a position in Oracle because I think the potential upside is large enough to justify the risk, but I would not invest heavily at this point.** I believe ORCL is undervalued relative to the long-term value of its cloud infrastructure business, and the recovery from the July 2026 low makes the risk/reward more attractive to me. The stock appears to be curling upward toward its mid-2025 trading levels while the underlying business continues to report strong OCI growth and a very large contracted backlog. **[Author judgment; operating and price evidence cited above.]**

At the same time, this is not a high-conviction, concentrated position. Fiscal 2026 included **77% cloud-infrastructure growth**, but it also required **\$55.663 billion of capital expenditures**, produced **negative \$23.686 billion of free cash flow**, and came with substantial additional borrowing and financing requirements. Those risks are significant enough that I would rather participate in the upside with a smaller position than make a large bet before Oracle proves that the infrastructure buildout can generate adequate returns. **[Sources: Oracle FY2026 Form 10-K, Item 7, pp. 44 and 54; Note 6, p. 82.]**

My investment thesis is therefore not that Oracle is risk-free or obviously cheap. It is that **the expected upside appears large enough that the position is worth taking despite the uncertainty**. I think the market price is below Oracle's intrinsic value, but I still need the DCF to quantify that difference. Until then, position sizing is how I account for the uncertainty: I am willing to own the stock, but I am not willing to make it one of my largest positions. **[Author judgment.]**

**What would change my call:** I would increase the position if a conservative DCF confirms substantial upside and Oracle demonstrates that its new cloud capacity can achieve strong utilization, returns above its cost of capital, and improving free cash flow. I would reduce or exit the position if OCI growth slows materially while capex, debt, dilution, or financing costs remain elevated, which would weaken the argument that the infrastructure investment is creating enough value for shareholders. **[Author judgment.]**
